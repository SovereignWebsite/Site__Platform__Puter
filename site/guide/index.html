How Puter.js reaches a model from the browser

Every fact below links to Puter's own documents or repository, with the date we read it. Anything we tested ourselves is labelled as ours. Anything Puter does not say is under What is not stated.

How it works

  1. The page includes Puter.js. Puter documents a script tag and an npm package, @heyputer/puter.js (Puter docs: Getting Started, read 25 November 2026).
  2. When the code first needs a cloud service, the visitor is asked to sign in with their Puter account. If they are already signed in, they are not asked again (Puter docs: Security and Permissions, read 25 November 2026).
  3. Sign-in opens a pop-up window. It must be triggered by a user action such as a click, because browsers block pop-ups that are not (Puter docs: auth.signIn(), read 25 November 2026).
  4. Once signed in, puter.ai.chat(prompt) returns a completion. The model is chosen with the model option (Puter docs: ai.chat(), read 25 November 2026).

There is no API key in the page and no backend of ours. Puter says the same of its own model: "No API keys at all" (Puter docs: User-Pays Model, read 25 November 2026).

Guest accounts

The sign-in call has an option, attempt_temp_user_creation, that lets Puter create a temporary user so a visitor can start without signing up. "They can always sign up later" (Puter docs: auth.signIn(), read 25 November 2026). Puter's rate limits give guests ("anonymous" accounts) their own, lower numbers (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026). Whether a fresh guest can finish a useful task is not something Puter's documents answer, and we have not measured it.

Models and features

  • If no model is named, ai.chat() defaults to gpt-5.4-nano. Puter says more than 500 models are available from providers including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Alibaba Cloud, xAI, Mistral and OpenRouter (Puter docs: ai.chat(), read 25 November 2026).
  • puter.ai.listModels() returns the models available to the app, with pricing and capability metadata where available. Each has an id and a provider (Puter docs: ai.listModels(), read 25 November 2026).
  • Options Puter lists for chat include stream, max_tokens, temperature and reasoning_effort (Puter docs: ai.chat(), read 25 November 2026).
  • The same AI budget is shared by chat, images, video, text to speech, speech to text and OCR (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).

Model lists and prices change. This guide does not copy a list; ask listModels() or Puter's model directory for the current one.

Who pays

  • Under Puter's User-Pays Model, users cover their own cloud and AI usage through their own Puter account. The developer's usage never mixes with theirs (Puter docs: User-Pays Model, read 25 November 2026).
  • Every user starts with a free monthly allowance. If it runs out, Puter prompts them to upgrade, or they can do it in their billing settings (Puter docs: User-Pays Model, read 25 November 2026).
  • The allowance resets monthly and does not roll over. Purchased credits never expire and are spent after the allowance runs out (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).
  • AI is "priced per model" and charged "at real cost". Rates can change (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).
  • A request that produced no output is free. A stream that stops early is charged on an estimate of what was streamed (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).
  • puter.auth.getMonthlyUsage() returns the user's monthly usage. Called from an app, usage and appTotals cover only that app, while allowanceInfo covers the whole account (Puter docs: auth.getMonthlyUsage(), read 25 November 2026). The demo can show your own result.

Puter's docs describe the arrangement as users paying for their own usage. This site therefore does not describe the service as free for visitors.

  • A chat completion counts as concurrent until the model finishes, even if the page disconnects, for up to 10 minutes (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).
  • Three independent checks can stop a request: usage credit (402 insufficient_funds), rate limit (429 too_many_requests) and storage quota (413) (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).
  • Several expensive requests started together reserve their maximum cost first, so later ones can fail with 402 even when the month's allowance is not spent (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026). Interfaces should not tell users "you have run out" on a 402 alone.
  • Limits include inputs: an image inlined in a chat message is capped at 5 MB, for example (Puter docs: Rate Limits and Quotas, read 25 November 2026).

Privacy and permissions

What signing in gives the page

After sign-in an app gets, by default: a folder in the user's cloud storage, its own key-value store, the AI services, and the ability to "create and publish websites on the user's behalf" (Puter docs: Security and Permissions, read 25 November 2026). An AI button needs only the third. Tell users the rest.

What Puter's privacy policy says

  • Its effective date is 18 July 2022 (Puter's privacy policy, read 25 November 2026).
  • Data may be stored, processed and transmitted "in Canada and locations around the world", including outside the user's country (Puter's privacy policy, read 25 November 2026).
  • Puter uses cookies and similar technologies, and uses third parties for hosting, analytics, customer care, marketing, advertising and payments (Puter's privacy policy, read 25 November 2026).
  • Ours, not Puter's: we searched the policy text on 25 November 2026 for "AI", "artificial" and "model" and found no match. The policy as published does not address prompts, model providers or training.

What we saw when the sign-in window opened

Tested by us on 25 November 2026, not stated in Puter's documents. In a clean headless browser, opening the sign-in window loaded scripts from gui-cdn.puter.com, Sentry, Plausible and Cloudflare Turnstile, and a favicon from Google. That window runs on Puter's own domain, under Puter's own page, not on this site. Our page made no request to any Puter host before the click, and wrote nothing to localStorage.

The script itself

Puter documents loading the script from https://js.puter.com/v2/ (Puter docs: Getting Started, read 25 November 2026). That address is a floating URL: Puter can change what it serves at any time, and the code runs with full rights on your page. A static page cannot pin it with a subresource integrity hash.

This site instead serves a pinned copy of the npm package @heyputer/puter.js version 2.6.5 from its own origin, checked against the package's published integrity hash. The page's content security policy then allows scripts from this site only. The record is in the README at site/vendor/puter.js-2.6.5/ in this site's repository. A pinned copy is a third-party script by design: we did not write it and we update it only on purpose.

The hosts the demo's policy allows besides this site are api.puter.com (the API) and puter.com (sign-in). We found the bundle names these hosts by searching its code and by running it; the full list, and what we have not tested after sign-in, is in the README.

What is not stated

Puter's documents we read on 25 November 2026 do not say:

  • How large the free monthly allowance is. The docs say it is "shown in the dashboard".
  • Which sign-in providers exist. Google is mentioned as an example only (Puter docs: auth.signIn(), read 25 November 2026).
  • Which company hosts each model upstream of Puter, how long prompts are kept, or whether they are used for training.
  • Whether the script can be pinned or checked with an integrity hash. (The npm package can be pinned, as above.)
  • How guest accounts behave in practice, or how sign-in behaves on every phone or browser.
  • Puter's developer agreement and terms: we have not read them for this site yet, so we make no claim about them.

An independent review for this site's owner reports several further details, for example that allowance amounts are in microcents and that Puter.js keeps its sign-in token in the page's localStorage. We have not checked those against Puter's documents, so we do not state them as fact here.

A safer pattern: keep the script in its own frame

If a page holds anything private, such as keys or other people's data, put Puter.js in a small page on a separate origin, embed it in a sandboxed iframe, and pass only the prompt text in and the answer out with postMessage, checking event.origin each way. The sign-in button then lives inside that frame, so the user's click opens the pop-up. This site does not do this: it needs a host of its own, and the demo holds no private data. We describe it as a pattern, not as something we have tested.
